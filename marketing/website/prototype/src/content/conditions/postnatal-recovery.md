Birth changes your body in ways that are not always visible — and recovery takes longer than the standard six-week check implies. Physiotherapy in the postnatal period helps you understand what has changed, what needs support, and how to rebuild strength safely.

## What changes after birth?

**Pelvic floor** — the pelvic floor muscles stretch significantly during vaginal birth (and are also affected by pregnancy itself, regardless of how you birth). You may experience leaking, urgency, pelvic pressure, or difficulty with bowel emptying.

**Abdominal wall** — a degree of diastasis recti (separation of the rectus abdominis muscles) is normal in pregnancy. After birth, the gap often closes on its own — but in some cases it persists and affects function, contributing to core weakness, back pain, and difficulty with loading.

**Pain and scar tissue** — perineal tears and episiotomies, caesarean scars, and the physical demands of caring for a newborn can all cause ongoing pain. Scar tissue responds well to physiotherapy.

**Posture and musculoskeletal changes** — carrying, feeding, and sleep deprivation place new demands on the neck, shoulders, and back. Pelvic and hip pain that began in pregnancy may also persist.

## When should I see a physio after birth?

You do not need to wait until your six-week check, though that is a reasonable time to start. You can be seen earlier if you have specific concerns — for example, bladder urgency, significant pain, or difficulty with basic movement.

Many women seek physiotherapy:
- Around 6–8 weeks postpartum for a postnatal assessment
- When returning to exercise, particularly impact activities or strength training
- When symptoms are interfering with daily life — leaking, pain, pressure
- Before or after any surgical procedure relating to prolapse or incontinence

## What does a postnatal physiotherapy assessment cover?

A Goji postnatal assessment typically includes:

- A full history, including your birth experience, infant feeding, and current concerns
- External assessment of posture, breathing pattern, and movement
- Abdominal assessment including diastasis recti evaluation
- Pelvic floor assessment (internal, with your informed consent — you can decline)
- Goal-setting and a plan for returning to activities you care about

## When can I return to running, exercise, or sport?

General guidance (not a substitute for an individual assessment):

- **Low-impact walking** — most people can resume this within days of birth, as tolerated
- **Abdominal strengthening (planks, sit-ups)** — not before 8–12 weeks postpartum, and ideally after a physiotherapy assessment
- **Running, jumping, and high-impact activity** — not before 12 weeks postpartum as a minimum; many people benefit from physiotherapy clearance before returning to these
- **Return to team sport or gym classes** — depends on your recovery; an assessment helps you return with confidence

The 12-week guideline is a minimum, not a guarantee. Symptoms (leaking, heaviness, pain) during or after exercise are signals to slow down, not push through.

## Diastasis recti — what you need to know

Diastasis recti (DR) is a widening of the gap between the two sides of the rectus abdominis muscle. A degree of separation is normal in pregnancy, and most close significantly in the weeks after birth.

DR is not measured solely by the size of the gap. What matters more is the *function* of the linea alba (the connective tissue at the midline) — whether it can transfer load effectively. A physiotherapist assesses this through palpation and functional testing, not just a measurement.

**Signs that DR may need attention:**
- A visible "coning" or "doming" along the midline with abdominal effort
- A persistent feeling of core weakness or instability
- Back pain associated with loading or exercise
- Difficulty with basic functional tasks

Most people with DR do not need surgery. Physiotherapy focused on load management, breathing, and progressive strengthening is effective for the majority.
