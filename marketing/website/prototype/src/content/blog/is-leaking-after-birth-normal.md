Short answer: leaking in the first few weeks after birth is common, and a fair amount of it settles by itself as the pelvic floor recovers. Leaking that is still happening at three months, or that is changing what you are willing to do, is worth getting assessed. It is not something you have to accept, and it is not something that reliably improves on its own once you are past that point.

The reason this matters is that a lot of women are told the opposite. "That's just what happens after babies" is one of the most common things we hear in the clinic, usually from someone who has been quietly managing it for years.

## Why does it happen in the first place?

Pregnancy and birth load the pelvic floor heavily. The muscles that help keep the urethra closed have been stretched, sometimes injured, and are working alongside abdominal muscles that have also changed shape. When you sneeze, cough, laugh, run or lift, pressure inside the abdomen rises sharply. If the pelvic floor cannot respond fast enough or firmly enough at that moment, some urine escapes.

That is stress urinary incontinence, and it is the most common form after birth. It is a timing and load problem as much as a strength problem, which is part of why simply doing more squeezes does not always fix it.

## What usually settles on its own?

In the first six to eight weeks, some improvement is expected. Tissue is healing, swelling is going down, and the muscles are gradually returning to a normal resting state. Waiting and watching during that window is reasonable.

What tends not to resolve by waiting:

- Leaking that is unchanged between six weeks and three months
- Leaking that only shows up when you go back to running or lifting
- A sense of heaviness or dragging in the vagina alongside the leaking
- Leaking together with urgency, where you get very little warning

## Are pelvic floor exercises enough on their own?

Sometimes, but not reliably, and not without knowing what you are working with first.

The complication is that not everyone leaking has a weak pelvic floor. Some people have an overactive one that never fully relaxes, and in that case a programme of hard squeezing can make symptoms worse rather than better. Others are contracting the wrong muscles altogether, or holding their breath, or bearing down when they think they are lifting.

An assessment establishes what your pelvic floor is actually doing before anyone prescribes exercises for it. That is the whole value of it: not a generic programme, but the right one.

## What happens at an assessment?

A first appointment is mostly conversation and observation. We take a bladder history, which means what you drink, how often you go, when the leaking happens and what sets it off. That alone usually distinguishes stress leaking from urgency leaking.

The physical part looks at how the pelvic floor contracts, whether it can relax again, how long it holds, and how it copes under load, so coughing, jumping or lifting depending on what is relevant to you. An internal vaginal assessment gives the clearest information and is offered, but it is not required, and plenty of useful assessment happens without it.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long after birth should I wait before getting assessed?

If leaking is your only concern and it is improving, six to eight weeks is a reasonable point to review. If it is not improving, or if there is pain, heaviness or a feeling of something bulging, there is no reason to wait. Assessment is appropriate at any stage after birth.

### Is it too late if I gave birth years ago?

No. Pelvic floor physiotherapy is a first-line treatment regardless of how long symptoms have been present. Many of the women we see had their children a decade or more ago.

### Does it only affect women who had a vaginal birth?

No. Pregnancy itself loads the pelvic floor, so leaking occurs after caesarean birth too. The delivery route changes some of the picture but does not determine whether symptoms appear.

### Can I keep running while I sort this out?

Usually yes, often with adjustments to distance, surface or timing while you build capacity. Stopping all loading is rarely the answer. Part of the assessment is working out what you can keep doing safely.
