Recovery after birth normally takes around six to twelve weeks for the basics: bleeding that is heavy at first and fades over a few weeks, a perineum or caesarean wound that heals, tiredness, and bladder, bowel and pelvic floor control that gradually return. In Singapore, KK Women's and Children's Hospital (KKH) gives a postnatal check-up appointment at 4-6 weeks after delivery ([KKH](https://www.kkh.com.sg/symptoms-treatments/preparing-for-an-elective-planned-caesarean-section)). Some symptoms are not part of normal recovery and need a doctor without delay, and those come first below.

## Which symptoms after birth need a doctor urgently?

NICE's postnatal care guideline and the NHS list these as signs of potentially serious problems ([NICE NG194, 1.2.4](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng194/chapter/Recommendations); [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/labour-and-birth/after-the-birth/your-body/)). Contact your doctor or hospital straight away, or go to the nearest A&E or call 995 if it feels like an emergency, if you have:

- Sudden or very heavy bleeding, bleeding that increases, or large clots, especially if you feel faint or your heart is racing.
- A fever, shivering, tummy, pelvic or perineal pain, or discharge with an unpleasant smell.
- Pain, swelling or redness in the calf of one leg.
- Chest pain, or difficulty breathing.
- A severe or persistent headache, changes in your vision, or vomiting.
- A caesarean or perineal wound that is more red, swollen or painful, leaks pus or foul-smelling fluid, or starts to open ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/caesarean-section/recovery/); [NICE NG194, 1.2.15](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng194/chapter/Recommendations)).
- A red, swollen area on the breast that lasts more than 24 hours despite self-care ([NICE NG194, 1.2.4](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng194/chapter/Recommendations)). See our page on [blocked ducts and mastitis](/conditions/blocked-ducts-mastitis/).
- Thoughts of suicide, or of harming yourself or your baby ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/mental-health/conditions/post-natal-depression/overview/)). Samaritans of Singapore has a 24-hour hotline on 1767 ([SOS](https://www.sos.org.sg/)). If anyone is in immediate danger, call 995 or go to the nearest A&E.

## What should I check off in the first twelve weeks?

Everyone recovers at a different pace, and a caesarean or a more complicated birth usually means a longer recovery. Use this as a guide to what is typical, not a timetable you have to meet.

| Area | First 2 weeks | 2-6 weeks | 6-12 weeks |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Bleeding (lochia) | Heavy at first; use maternity pads, not tampons. It can be heavier and redder while breastfeeding. | Gradually lighter and browner. | Usually stopped. KKH advises contacting your doctor if bleeding is excessive or lasts more than 6-8 weeks. |
| Perineum or caesarean wound | Bathe stitches daily with plain warm water and pat dry. Keep a caesarean wound clean and dry. | Skin is healing. After a caesarean, lift nothing heavier than your baby until you are comfortable. | Once fully healed, scar care can begin. See [Caesarean recovery](/conditions/caesarean-recovery/) and [perineal tears](/conditions/perineal-tear/). |
| Bladder and bowel | Drink plenty of water, eat fibre and avoid straining. Tell your midwife or doctor if it is hard to pee. | Some leaking when you cough or laugh is common. Mention it at your postnatal check. | Leaking that continues around 3 months after birth is worth an assessment. |
| Pelvic floor | Gentle squeezes once comfortable. See [pelvic floor exercises after birth](/blog/pelvic-floor-exercises-after-birth/). | Build up holds and repeats slowly. | Keep going, and get advice before high-impact exercise. |
| Feeding posture, wrists and neck | Sit with your back well supported, a small cushion behind your lower back and your feet on the floor. | Change nappies on a raised surface, and bend your knees to lift. | Wrist, thumb, neck or shoulder pain that is not settling is worth getting checked. |
| Walking and movement | Get up and move around; short walks help reduce the risk of blood clots. | Gentle exercise as you feel up to it, after a straightforward birth. | Higher-impact exercise after advice at your postnatal check. |
| Sleep and mood | Rest when you can. Tearfulness in the first days (baby blues) is common. | Low mood lasting beyond 2 weeks needs a doctor's advice. | Postnatal depression can start any time in the first year. |
| Check-ups | Follow your hospital's discharge advice and appointments. | Postnatal check-up at 4-6 weeks. | Follow up anything raised at the check. |

Sources for the table: bleeding, stitches, bladder, bowel and feeding posture from the [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/labour-and-birth/after-the-birth/your-body/) and [NHS post-pregnancy body](https://www.nhs.uk/baby/support-and-services/your-post-pregnancy-body/) pages; caesarean wound, lifting, bleeding duration and check-up timing from [KKH](https://www.kkh.com.sg/symptoms-treatments/preparing-for-an-elective-planned-caesarean-section); walking and exercise from the [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/baby/support-and-services/keeping-fit-and-healthy-with-a-baby/) and [NHS caesarean recovery](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/caesarean-section/recovery/); mood from [KKH](https://www.kkh.com.sg/symptoms-treatments/postnatal-depression-mental-wellness) and the [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/mental-health/conditions/post-natal-depression/overview/).

## When is the postnatal check in Singapore, and what should I raise?

KKH gives patients a postnatal check-up appointment at 4-6 weeks after delivery ([KKH](https://www.kkh.com.sg/symptoms-treatments/preparing-for-an-elective-planned-caesarean-section)). If you delivered elsewhere, your obstetrician or polyclinic will tell you when yours is. NICE describes the check at 6-8 weeks as a chance to review your physical and mental health and respond to any concerns ([NICE NG194, 1.2.7](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng194/chapter/Recommendations)).

It helps to write a list before you go, because appointments can feel rushed with a baby in the room. Things worth mentioning:

- Bleeding that has not settled, or has come back heavier.
- A wound or stitches that are still sore.
- Leaking urine, wind or stool, or a feeling of heaviness in the vagina.
- A gap or bulge down the middle of your tummy. The NHS advises seeing your doctor if the gap is still obvious 8 weeks after birth ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/baby/support-and-services/your-post-pregnancy-body/)). Our [diastasis recti](/conditions/diastasis-recti/) page explains why the gap is only part of the picture.
- Pain with sex, or worries about it.
- Contraception. The NHS notes that you can get pregnant again from 3 weeks after birth, even if you are breastfeeding ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/pregnancy/labour-and-birth/after-the-birth/your-body/)).
- How you are feeling, honestly.

## What about low mood and postnatal depression?

Feeling tearful or low for a few days after birth is common and is often called the baby blues. KKH advises seeking professional advice if it lasts beyond two weeks, and estimates that around 3-5% of women experience a moderate-to-severe depression that needs medical attention ([KKH](https://www.kkh.com.sg/symptoms-treatments/postnatal-depression-mental-wellness)).

Postnatal depression is a recognised illness, not a weakness, and it usually gets better with treatment. Signs include low mood, finding it hard to enjoy anything, feeling you cannot cope, constant worry, difficulty bonding with your baby, and trouble sleeping even when you get the chance. It can start during pregnancy or any time in the first year after birth ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/mental-health/conditions/post-natal-depression/overview/)). Speak to your GP, polyclinic doctor or obstetrician; you do not need to wait for your postnatal check. If you have thoughts of harming yourself or your baby, use the urgent options above.

## Where does a pelvic health physiotherapist fit in?

A pelvic health physiotherapist does not replace your doctor or your postnatal check. Physiotherapy can help with the physical side of recovery: leaking, heaviness, pelvic floor technique, caesarean or perineal scar discomfort, tummy separation, back, wrist or neck pain from feeding and carrying, and planning a return to exercise.

At Goji, [postpartum physiotherapy](/services/postpartum-physiotherapy/) is suitable after vaginal or caesarean birth, at any stage, and you do not need a doctor's referral. You can come after your six-week postnatal review, or earlier if something is bothering you. The [Mummy and Baby assessment](/services/mummy-baby-assessment/) combines your recovery check with a look at your baby's movement and development, for babies up to 12 months. If returning to exercise is your main question, see our post on [returning to exercise after birth](/blog/returning-to-exercise-after-birth/).

## Frequently asked questions

### When can I drive after a caesarean?

KKH advises not driving until you have fully recovered and your wound has completely healed, which may take up to six weeks ([KKH](https://www.kkh.com.sg/symptoms-treatments/preparing-for-an-elective-planned-caesarean-section)). Check with your doctor, and your insurer, if you are unsure.

### When can I go swimming after giving birth?

The NHS suggests waiting until 7 days after your postnatal bleeding has stopped ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/baby/support-and-services/keeping-fit-and-healthy-with-a-baby/)). If you had a caesarean or stitches, make sure the wound has fully healed first.

### When is it safe to have sex again?

There is no fixed date. KKH's advice after a caesarean is to wait until you feel comfortable, and notes that it is normal to take weeks before you are ready ([KKH](https://www.kkh.com.sg/symptoms-treatments/preparing-for-an-elective-planned-caesarean-section)). After a tear, your obstetrician can confirm healing. Pain with sex that does not ease is worth raising with your doctor or a pelvic health physiotherapist.

### Do I need a postnatal check if I feel fine?

Yes. It is a good time to talk about any physical or mental health problems you have had since the birth, including ones you have been putting off ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/baby/support-and-services/your-post-pregnancy-body/)).
