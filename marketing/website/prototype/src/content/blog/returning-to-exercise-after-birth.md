The six-week check is a medical clearance, not a starting gun. It tells you that healing is on track. It does not tell you that your pelvic floor and abdominal wall are ready for running, jumping or heavy lifting, because it does not assess them under load.

What follows is an illustration of how a straightforward return to exercise can unfold. Every birth and every body is different, and a physiotherapist sets the actual pace with you. Treat it as a map rather than a timetable.

## Weeks 0 to 6: less than you think, on purpose

The work in this window is unglamorous: breathing that coordinates with the pelvic floor, gentle activation, walking that builds gradually, and getting positions for feeding and lifting sorted so you are not spending all day loaded badly.

Two things people underestimate here. First, walking counts and progresses faster than expected if you build it steadily. Second, sleep and feeding load matter to recovery as much as any exercise does, and a plan that ignores them does not survive contact with a newborn.

## Around week 6: get assessed, then decide

This is the useful point for a postpartum physiotherapy assessment. It looks at the pelvic floor's ability to contract and relax, the abdominal wall including any separation, how your breathing and pressure management work, and how all of that behaves when load goes up.

That assessment is what turns a generic timeline into your timeline. Someone with good pelvic floor control and no symptoms progresses differently from someone with a heaviness sensation or a persistent gap, and it is not possible to tell which you are without looking.

## Weeks 6 to 12: build capacity before impact

The general shape here is strength work first, impact later. Progressive resistance training, loaded carries, single-leg work, and gradually heavier compound movements build the capacity that running demands.

Impact tends to come back into the picture once you can manage things like single-leg balance and control, repeated hopping without symptoms, and a walking volume that is comfortable. Those are common markers rather than fixed rules, and they get tested rather than assumed.

Reformer Pilates and structured strength sessions both fit well in this window, which is why we run [Clinical Pilates](/services/clinical-pilates) alongside physiotherapy.

## Signs to slow down

Regardless of which week you are in, these are reasons to pull back and get reviewed:

- Leaking that appears or worsens with an activity
- A heaviness, dragging or bulging sensation in the vagina
- Pelvic or low back pain that builds through a session or the day after
- Doming or coning along the midline of the abdomen during effort
- Bleeding that increases after you have been progressing

None of these mean something has gone wrong permanently. They mean the load is currently ahead of the capacity, and the answer is usually to adjust rather than to stop everything.

## Frequently asked questions

### When can I start running again?

Most guidance points to somewhere from around twelve weeks postpartum at the earliest, and only once you can load the pelvic floor without symptoms. The date matters far less than the readiness, and an assessment is how you establish the second one.

### Is it too late to start if my baby is already one?

No. There is no window that closes. Rebuilding strength and pelvic floor function is worth doing whenever you get to it, and plenty of the women we see start much later than twelve weeks.

### Does a caesarean birth change the timeline?

It changes the early weeks, since there is abdominal wound healing to respect, but pregnancy has loaded the pelvic floor regardless of delivery route. Assessment is just as relevant.

### Can I exercise while breastfeeding?

Yes. Feed or express beforehand if comfort is an issue, and keep fluid intake up. Breastfeeding is not a reason to avoid strength training.

### What about diastasis recti?

Abdominal separation is normal in late pregnancy and narrows for most people in the months afterwards. What matters more than the width of the gap is how well you can generate tension across it, which is what an assessment looks at.
