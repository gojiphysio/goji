## Short answer block (place directly under H1 — AI citation target)

Post-partum physiotherapy helps women recover physically after birth — whether vaginal or Caesarean. A physiotherapist will assess your pelvic floor, abdominal muscles, posture, and any areas of pain or discomfort, and guide you back to the activities you want to return to, at a pace that makes sense for your body and your stage of recovery.

## Body content

### What happens to your body in the weeks after birth?

Birth — however it happens — places significant demands on your body. The pelvic floor muscles stretch and adapt throughout pregnancy and are directly involved in a vaginal birth. After a Caesarean, the abdominal wall and supporting structures go through surgical repair and healing. Either way, your body has done something extraordinary, and it needs time and the right kind of support to recover well.

Common experiences in the weeks and months after birth include:

- **Pelvic floor changes:** leaking when you cough, sneeze, laugh, or exercise; urgency to reach the toilet; reduced sensation.
- **Abdominal muscle separation (diastasis recti):** a gap between the two sides of the rectus abdominis that commonly widens during pregnancy. It does not always close on its own.
- **Pain:** in the lower back, pelvis, coccyx (tailbone), hips, or perineum, especially when lifting, walking long distances, or caring for a newborn.
- **Scar tissue sensitivity:** after an episiotomy, perineal tear, or Caesarean scar.
- **Posture and neck/shoulder tension:** from feeding, carrying, and the demands of early parenthood.

Not everything that feels uncomfortable after birth is abnormal — but not everything should be waited out, either.

### When should you see a physio after having a baby?

There is no single "right" time, but most physiotherapists recommend an initial post-natal assessment around **six weeks after a vaginal birth** or **eight weeks after a Caesarean**, once your medical clearance is given. If you have significant discomfort, leaking, or pain before that, it is worth reaching out sooner — a physiotherapist can advise on what is safe and appropriate for your stage.

If you are planning to return to exercise, run, or resume sport, a physio assessment before you start back helps you do so safely.

### What does postnatal physiotherapy involve?

Your first session at Goji starts with a conversation: your birth experience, your current concerns, your goals, and what you want to be able to do. The assessment then typically includes:

- **Pelvic floor assessment** — internal or external, depending on your preference, comfort, and clinical need. This checks muscle strength, coordination, and function, not just "can you squeeze".
- **Diastasis recti assessment** — checking the degree of separation and, importantly, the function and load capacity of the abdominal muscles.
- **Musculoskeletal check** — lower back, pelvis, hips, and posture, particularly if you are carrying or feeding a baby for many hours each day.
- **Movement and load assessment** — for those returning to exercise, a simple movement screen to identify what you are ready for.

At the end of your session, you will leave with a clear picture of what is happening and a practical plan for what comes next — not just a sheet of exercises.

### What is diastasis recti, and can physiotherapy help?

Diastasis recti — sometimes called "mummy tummy" — is a separation of the two sides of the rectus abdominis muscle at the linea alba. It is present to some degree in most pregnant women by the third trimester; what matters is the function and load capacity of the abdominal wall, not just the gap measurement.

Physiotherapy can help by:

- Accurately assessing the degree and type of separation.
- Guiding a progressive return to load and activity that does not worsen the separation.
- Teaching you which exercises are appropriate (and which to avoid or modify) at each stage.
- Working on the underlying trunk and pelvic floor coordination that supports recovery.

"Just do your core exercises" is rarely the whole story. The right exercises for your diastasis depend on where you are in recovery.

### When can I safely return to exercise after birth?

Returning to exercise after birth is not a single milestone — it is a progression that depends on how your body has recovered, what exercise you are returning to, and how you feel. Running and high-impact exercise typically require a longer recovery than walking or light strengthening.

General guidance from sports physiotherapy and women's health research suggests waiting at least 12 weeks before returning to high-impact activity, but this is a minimum, not a guarantee. A physiotherapy assessment gives you a clearer, individual picture of your readiness.

If you are experiencing any leaking, heaviness, or pelvic pressure during or after exercise — that is a signal to get assessed before increasing load.

## FAQ section

**Is it normal to leak urine after having a baby?**
Leaking when you cough, sneeze, laugh, or exercise (called stress urinary incontinence) is common after childbirth, but it is not something you have to accept as permanent. Pelvic floor physiotherapy can assess why leaking is occurring and help you address it. If leaking is affecting your confidence or daily life, it is worth getting assessed sooner rather than waiting to see if it resolves on its own.

**How soon after birth can I see a physio?**
For general assessment and guidance, you can typically see a physiotherapist from around six weeks after a vaginal birth or eight weeks after a Caesarean, following your postnatal check. If you have significant pain, leaking, or concerns before that, reach out — a physiotherapist can advise on what is appropriate for your stage of recovery even before your formal clearance appointment.

**Does it matter whether I had a vaginal birth or a Caesarean?**
Both types of birth warrant a postnatal physiotherapy assessment, though the focus differs. Vaginal birth often involves pelvic floor loading and potential perineal trauma; Caesarean birth involves an abdominal incision, core recovery, and scar management. Your physiotherapist will tailor the assessment and plan to your specific birth experience.

**What is the pelvic floor, and why does it matter after birth?**
The pelvic floor is a group of muscles, ligaments, and connective tissue that sit at the base of the pelvis, supporting the bladder, bowel, and uterus. During pregnancy and birth, these structures are placed under significant load. After birth, they may need specific rehabilitation to regain function — including coordination, strength, and the ability to relax, not just contract.

**Can postnatal physiotherapy help with pelvic girdle pain that has continued after birth?**
Yes. Pelvic girdle pain (PGP) during pregnancy sometimes persists or changes in character after birth. A physiotherapy assessment can identify what is contributing — whether muscle weakness, joint load, posture, or activity demands — and guide a recovery plan. Most cases of postnatal PGP respond well to physiotherapy.

**Do I need a doctor's referral to see a physiotherapist?**
No referral is needed to book a physiotherapy appointment at Goji. If you have private insurance that requires a referral for reimbursement, check your policy before your first visit.
