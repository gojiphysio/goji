## Body content

### What causes lower back pain?

Lower back pain has many possible causes, which is part of why it can be hard to manage without a proper assessment. Common contributors include:

- **Muscle and joint strain:** from a sudden movement, lifting, or prolonged time in one position.
- **Disc-related pain:** when the intervertebral discs are under load or pressure, sometimes contributing to pain that radiates into the buttock or leg (commonly called sciatica).
- **Posture and load habits:** sustained sitting or standing postures that place uneven load on the spine — a very common pattern in desk-based work.
- **Pregnancy-related back pain:** the combination of postural changes, ligament relaxation, and load redistribution during pregnancy can cause significant lower back discomfort.
- **Post-partum changes:** changes in core and pelvic floor coordination after birth can affect spinal load and stability.
- **Sports and activity demands:** repetitive loading, inadequate warm-up, or returning to exercise too quickly after rest.
- **Persistent or recurring pain:** sometimes back pain that has been present for months or years involves more complex patterns — sensitivity, movement habits, and load tolerance — that benefit from a structured rehabilitation approach.

### When should you see a physiotherapist for back pain?

A physiotherapy assessment is helpful if your back pain:

- Has been present for more than a few days without improving.
- Is affecting your sleep, movement, or ability to go about your day.
- Is recurring — coming back every few months or with certain activities.
- Is accompanied by radiating pain, numbness, or tingling down the leg.
- Developed during or after pregnancy.
- Came on after a fall, accident, or sudden movement.

A physiotherapist will also advise you if your presentation warrants further investigation or medical review.

**Red flags to be aware of:** if your back pain is accompanied by loss of bladder or bowel control, weakness in both legs, or severe pain following trauma, seek medical attention promptly.

### What does a back pain assessment involve?

Your first appointment starts with a detailed conversation: when the pain started, what makes it better or worse, what you have already tried, and what you want to be able to do. This history is often the most important part of understanding what is going on.

The physical assessment typically includes:

- **Movement and posture analysis:** how the spine, hips, and surrounding structures move and load.
- **Neurological screen:** checking sensation, reflexes, and nerve function if there is radiating pain.
- **Palpation:** identifying areas of tenderness or stiffness in the muscles, joints, or soft tissue.
- **Functional tests:** movements or activities that reproduce your symptoms, so we understand what is driving them.
- **Load assessment:** for active people or those returning to exercise — what you can and cannot currently tolerate.

At the end of the session, you will leave with a clear explanation of what is likely contributing to your pain and a practical plan for what comes next.

### Can physiotherapy help with back pain during pregnancy?

Yes. Lower back pain during pregnancy is extremely common — affecting up to 70% of women — and it is one of the most common reasons pregnant women see a physiotherapist. Causes include the postural shift from a growing abdomen, relaxin-related ligament changes, and changes to the way load is distributed through the pelvis and spine.

Physiotherapy during pregnancy focuses on:

- Identifying what is driving your particular back pain (not all pregnancy back pain is the same).
- Practical movement strategies and posture adjustments.
- Appropriate exercise to support the back and pelvis through pregnancy.
- Differentiating between lower back pain and pelvic girdle pain — they often coexist but respond differently to treatment.

Treatment is safe during pregnancy and adapted to your trimester and comfort.

### Will I need X-rays or scans?

Most back pain does not require imaging, and a physiotherapy assessment alone can provide a clear picture of what is contributing to your symptoms. Scans are most useful when there is clinical reason to suspect structural pathology, neurological compromise, or when symptoms are not responding as expected.

Your physiotherapist will advise you if imaging is likely to be helpful in your case, and can work alongside findings from your GP or specialist.

**How do I know if my back pain needs physiotherapy, or if I should just rest?**
Short rest (one to two days) can be helpful immediately after an acute injury, but prolonged rest tends to slow recovery. Staying gently active — in whatever way is comfortable — is generally better. If pain is affecting your movement, sleep, or daily activities, or if it does not start to ease within a few days, a physiotherapy assessment is a useful next step.

**Is movement safe when I have back pain?**
In most cases, yes — and often better than the alternative. Movement helps reduce pain sensitivity, maintains flexibility, and supports recovery. That said, not all movements are appropriate for all back pain presentations. A physiotherapist can help you understand what to do and what to avoid for your specific situation.

**Can a physio help with sciatica?**
Sciatica — pain, numbness, or tingling that radiates from the lower back into the buttock and leg — often responds well to physiotherapy. Treatment depends on what is driving the nerve irritation: it might focus on movement, posture, load management, or specific exercises to help the nerve move more freely. Severe or rapidly worsening neurological symptoms should be assessed urgently.

**How many sessions will I need for back pain?**
This varies by individual and by how long the pain has been present. Acute back pain often responds within four to six sessions; long-standing or recurring pain may require a longer course with a rehabilitation focus. Your physiotherapist will give you a realistic sense of the timeline at your first appointment, and review regularly as you progress.

**What is the difference between seeing a physiotherapist and a chiropractor for back pain?**
Both professions work with musculoskeletal pain, including back pain. Physiotherapy has a strong evidence base for both manual therapy (hands-on treatment) and exercise rehabilitation — the combination of the two tends to produce better long-term outcomes than either alone. Your physiotherapist will advise on what approach is most likely to help your particular presentation.

**Can I exercise if I have back pain?**
Usually yes — with appropriate guidance on what to do and how to pace it. Exercise is one of the most effective long-term treatments for back pain. The key is choosing movements that are appropriate for your current stage of recovery and building load gradually. Your physiotherapist will guide this, including what to avoid while you are still in an acute phase.
