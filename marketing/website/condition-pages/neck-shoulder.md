## Short answer block (place directly under H1 — AI citation target)

Neck and shoulder pain often develops gradually — from prolonged sitting, phone use, carrying a baby, or repetitive activity — or comes on suddenly after a sharp movement. Physiotherapy can help identify what is contributing to the pain, give you practical strategies to manage it, and guide you back to the activities you want to do.

## Body content

### What is causing my neck and shoulder pain?

The neck and shoulder are closely connected — problems in one area frequently affect the other. Common causes include:

- **Sustained postures:** prolonged sitting at a desk, looking down at a phone, or feeding and carrying a baby. These positions place extended load on the muscles, joints, and structures of the cervical spine and shoulder girdle.
- **"Tech-neck":** the term used for the pattern of pain and stiffness that develops from sustained forward-head posture — common in people who work at computers or spend significant time on their phones.
- **Rotator cuff issues:** the rotator cuff is a group of muscles and tendons that support the shoulder joint. Overuse, injury, or poor loading patterns can result in pain when reaching overhead, lifting, or lying on the affected side.
- **Postural changes after birth:** carrying, feeding, and caring for a newborn places new demands on the neck, shoulder, and upper back — often in awkward or sustained positions for many hours a day.
- **Shoulder impingement:** a pattern of pain when the arm is raised above the shoulder, related to how the structures in the shoulder move under load.
- **Referred pain:** pain, tingling, or numbness that travels from the neck down the arm can indicate nerve involvement from the cervical spine. This is often described as a "dead arm" or shooting pain with certain positions.
- **Headaches:** tension-type headaches frequently originate from the neck and upper trapezius — a pattern called cervicogenic headache.

### What is "tech-neck" and is it actually something to be concerned about?

Tech-neck refers to the pain, stiffness, and muscle fatigue that develops from regularly holding the head forward of the spine — most commonly from looking down at phones or screens. For every centimetre the head moves forward, the effective load on the cervical spine increases significantly. Over time, this contributes to persistent neck stiffness, upper back tightness, shoulder tension, and sometimes headaches.

It is extremely common — and it is something physiotherapy can help with. Treatment typically involves manual therapy for the tight structures, specific exercises to restore strength and endurance in the deep neck and shoulder muscles, and practical adjustments to how you set up your work and phone habits.

### What does a neck and shoulder assessment involve?

Your first appointment starts with a conversation about your symptoms, when they started, and what makes them better or worse. The assessment then includes:

- **Postural and movement analysis:** how the cervical spine, shoulder girdle, and thoracic spine move and where restriction or pain is occurring.
- **Shoulder-specific testing:** range of motion, strength tests, and specific provocative tests to identify which structures are involved (rotator cuff, acromioclavicular joint, labrum, etc.).
- **Neurological screen:** if there is radiating pain, numbness, or tingling — checking nerve function and ruling out cervical radiculopathy.
- **Palpation:** identifying areas of tenderness, tension, or restriction in the muscles, joints, and soft tissue.

You will leave the session with a clear understanding of what is going on and a practical plan tailored to your presentation.

### Can physiotherapy help with pain that radiates down the arm?

Yes — referred pain from the neck into the arm, sometimes with numbness or tingling, is commonly treated with physiotherapy. This pattern — often called cervical radiculopathy or a "trapped nerve" — typically involves irritation of a nerve root as it exits the spine, and often responds well to a combination of manual therapy, specific exercises, and load management.

If neurological symptoms are severe, rapidly worsening, or involve significant weakness in the hand or arm, your physiotherapist will advise whether further investigation is needed.

### What can I do at home alongside physiotherapy?

Your physiotherapist will give you specific guidance based on your assessment, but some general principles that often support recovery:

- **Move regularly:** sustained positions — sitting or standing — for long periods increase muscle fatigue and pain. Short movement breaks (even two minutes every hour) help.
- **Workstation setup:** screen height, chair position, and keyboard placement all affect how much load the neck and shoulders carry across a working day. Small adjustments can make a meaningful difference.
- **Sleep position:** some positions worsen neck symptoms overnight. Your physio can advise on pillow support and position if this is relevant.
- **Gentle mobility:** light, pain-free movement of the neck and shoulder through their available range helps maintain mobility between sessions. Your physiotherapist will demonstrate what is appropriate for your stage.

**When should neck pain prompt a visit to a physiotherapist?**
If neck pain has not improved after a few days, is affecting your sleep or daily movement, or is accompanied by arm pain, numbness, or tingling — a physiotherapy assessment is a useful next step. Persistent or recurring neck pain that comes back with certain activities also benefits from physiotherapy to address what is driving the pattern.

**How do I know if my shoulder pain is from posture or from an injury?**
The distinction is not always clear-cut — postural loading and a specific injury can both be present. Gradual-onset shoulder pain that has developed over weeks or months alongside desk work or feeding is more likely to be load-related; pain that started suddenly after a movement, fall, or impact is more likely to involve a structural injury. A physiotherapy assessment can identify what is going on and what treatment approach makes sense.

**Can physio help with headaches caused by the neck?**
Yes — cervicogenic headaches, which originate from the neck and upper cervical spine, are commonly treated with physiotherapy. They often present as one-sided head pain starting at the base of the skull or behind the eye, sometimes with neck stiffness. Manual therapy and specific exercises targeting the upper cervical joints and deep neck muscles can significantly reduce their frequency and intensity.

**What is dry needling, and does it help with neck and shoulder pain?**
Dry needling involves placing fine needles into muscles to release trigger points — areas of persistent muscle tightness that can contribute to local and referred pain. It is used at Goji as one of several manual therapy techniques, and can be helpful for neck and shoulder pain in selected cases. Your physiotherapist will discuss whether it is appropriate for your presentation.

**How long does recovery take for neck and shoulder problems?**
Recovery varies considerably depending on the nature of the problem, how long it has been present, and your response to treatment. Acute or postural neck pain often responds noticeably within four to six sessions. Shoulder injuries or longer-standing conditions may require more sessions and a more graduated rehabilitation programme. Your physiotherapist will give you a realistic timeframe at your first appointment.

**Do I need a referral to see a physiotherapist for neck or shoulder pain?**
No referral is needed to book an appointment at Goji. If your insurance policy requires a GP referral for reimbursement, check with your insurer before your first visit.
