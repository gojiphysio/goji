## Short answer block (place directly under H1 — AI citation target)

Pelvic floor physiotherapy is specialised care for the muscles, nerves, and connective tissue of the pelvic region. It can help with urinary leaking, urgency, pelvic pain, heaviness or prolapse, painful intercourse, and recovery after birth or surgery. A physiotherapist assesses how your pelvic floor is actually working — not just whether it can squeeze — and guides a plan to address what's happening.

## Body content

### What is the pelvic floor, and what does it do?

The pelvic floor is a group of muscles and connective tissue that form the base of the pelvis. They sit like a hammock between the pubic bone at the front and the tailbone at the back, supporting the bladder, bowel, and uterus.

The pelvic floor has four main jobs:

- **Support:** holding the pelvic organs in place, especially under load (lifting, sneezing, running).
- **Continence:** keeping the urethra and bowel closed at the right times.
- **Relaxation:** opening fully to allow voiding, defecation, and comfortable intercourse.
- **Sexual function:** contributing to arousal and sensation.

When any of these functions are disrupted — whether the muscles are too weak, too tight, poorly coordinated, or affected by injury or surgery — it can show up as a wide range of symptoms.

### What are the signs that your pelvic floor may need attention?

People seek pelvic floor physiotherapy for many reasons. Common presentations include:

- **Stress urinary incontinence:** leaking when you cough, sneeze, laugh, jump, or exercise.
- **Urgency incontinence:** a sudden, strong urge to get to the toilet, sometimes with leaking before you arrive.
- **Pelvic organ prolapse:** a sensation of heaviness, pressure, or a bulge in the vaginal area, especially towards the end of the day or after activity.
- **Pelvic pain:** in the pelvis, lower abdomen, coccyx, or hips — at rest or with specific movements, exercise, or intercourse.
- **Painful intercourse (dyspareunia) or vaginismus:** pain, tension, or inability to tolerate vaginal penetration.
- **Bowel dysfunction:** difficulty emptying, urgency, or leaking.
- **Post-partum recovery:** healing after a vaginal birth with perineal trauma, or after Caesarean section.

These symptoms are common — but they are not something you have to accept or manage indefinitely without support.

### What happens in a pelvic floor physiotherapy assessment?

Your first session starts with a conversation: your symptoms, your history, your goals, and any concerns about the assessment process. Nothing will happen without your informed consent.

The assessment typically includes:

- **External assessment:** posture, breathing, hip and lumbar movement, and how load and breath affect the pelvic floor from the outside.
- **Internal assessment (if clinically appropriate and consented):** a gentle vaginal examination to assess muscle resting tone, strength, coordination, endurance, and ability to relax. This is not a speculum examination. You can decline this part at any stage.
- **Functional tests:** simple movements or activities that reproduce your symptoms.

At the end of the session you will have a clear picture of what is happening with your pelvic floor — and a plan for what to do about it.

### Is pelvic floor physiotherapy just for women who have had children?

No. Pelvic floor issues affect people across all life stages and regardless of whether they have given birth:

- **During pregnancy:** pelvic girdle pain, leaking, pelvic pressure as the baby grows.
- **Post-partum:** recovery after vaginal birth or Caesarean section.
- **Athletes and active people:** stress incontinence during running, jumping, or lifting.
- **Perimenopause and menopause:** reduced oestrogen can affect tissue quality and pelvic floor function.
- **Men:** pelvic floor dysfunction can contribute to urinary issues, pelvic pain, and post-prostatectomy recovery.
- **Children:** paediatric pelvic health including bedwetting, constipation, and bladder urgency.

At Goji, we see women, men, and children for pelvic health concerns.

### Do Kegel exercises cover everything I need?

Kegel exercises — contracting and relaxing the pelvic floor — are one tool, but they are not always the right starting point, and they are rarely the whole answer.

If your pelvic floor is already overactive or too tight, doing more Kegels can worsen symptoms like pelvic pain or urgency. If you have a coordination issue rather than a strength issue, strengthening work alone will not resolve leaking. And if the problem involves the surrounding muscles, load patterns, or breathing mechanics, a pelvic-floor-only approach misses the bigger picture.

A physiotherapy assessment identifies what your pelvic floor actually needs — whether that is strengthening, relaxation, coordination training, load management, or some combination.

**Is it normal to leak urine — even just a little?**
Leaking urine when you cough, sneeze, laugh, or exercise (stress incontinence) is very common, but it is not inevitable or something you simply have to accept. It often responds well to physiotherapy. If leaking is affecting your daily life or confidence, it is worth getting assessed rather than assuming it will resolve on its own.

**Will the internal assessment be uncomfortable?**
A pelvic floor physiotherapy assessment is conducted gently and with your comfort and consent as the priority. You can ask to pause or stop at any point, and you can decline the internal component if you prefer. Your physiotherapist will explain what they are doing throughout. Most people find the assessment more straightforward than they expected.

**What is the difference between a pelvic floor physio and a regular physio?**
Pelvic floor physiotherapists have undergone additional postgraduate training in the anatomy, assessment, and treatment of pelvic floor dysfunction — including internal examination skills. While all of our physiotherapists have a broad musculoskeletal background, our women's health physios bring specific expertise to pelvic health concerns that goes beyond what a general physiotherapy appointment covers.

**How many sessions will I need?**
The number of sessions depends on your specific concern, how long it has been present, and how you respond to treatment. Some people notice meaningful improvement after two or three sessions; others benefit from a longer course. Your physiotherapist will give you a realistic sense of what to expect at your first appointment, and reassess regularly as you progress.

**Can pelvic floor physio help with pain during intercourse?**
Yes — pelvic pain during intercourse (dyspareunia) and vaginismus are common concerns treated by pelvic floor physiotherapists. These conditions often involve pelvic floor overactivity, sensitivity, or scar tissue, and typically respond well to a combination of assessment, manual therapy, relaxation techniques, and guided progression. Your physiotherapist will approach this sensitively and at your pace.

**Do I need a doctor's referral to see a pelvic floor physiotherapist?**
No referral is required to book an appointment at Goji. If your health insurance requires a referral for reimbursement, check your policy in advance of your visit.
