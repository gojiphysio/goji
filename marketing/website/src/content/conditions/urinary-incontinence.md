Urinary incontinence is any unintentional leakage of urine, and pelvic floor physiotherapy is the first-line treatment recommended by international guidelines for its most common forms. The [NICE guideline on urinary incontinence in women (NG123)](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng123/chapter/Recommendations) recommends at least 3 months of supervised pelvic floor muscle training first for stress or mixed leakage, and bladder training for at least 6 weeks first for urge leakage. Surgery is considered for stress incontinence when non-surgical treatment has not worked. You can book directly; no doctor's referral is needed.

Incontinence is among the most common and most treatable conditions in women's health. In Singapore, [about 15% of women have stress urinary incontinence](https://www.kkh.com.sg/symptoms-treatments/stress-urinary-incontinence-female-urinary-disorders), according to KK Women's and Children's Hospital. It affects women of all ages, not just those who have given birth. Yet many women do not seek treatment, assuming it is an inevitable consequence of childbirth or ageing. It is not.

## What are the different types of urinary incontinence?

The most clinically relevant types for women are:

- Stress urinary incontinence (SUI): leakage that occurs with increased intra-abdominal pressure - coughing, sneezing, laughing, running, jumping, or lifting. The underlying mechanism is insufficient urethral closure when the pelvic floor is rapidly loaded.

- Urgency urinary incontinence (UUI): leakage associated with a sudden, strong urge to urinate that is difficult to defer. Often involves bladder overactivity. KKH describes the wider overactive bladder picture as urgency, [passing urine more than 7 times during the day](https://www.kkh.com.sg/symptoms-treatments/physiotherapy-for-overactive-bladder-female-urinary-disorders), getting up at night to pass urine, and leaking before reaching a toilet.

- Mixed urinary incontinence: a combination of stress and urgency incontinence, which is common.

- Overflow incontinence: incomplete bladder emptying leading to dribbling or constant leakage. Less common in women; requires medical assessment.

Understanding which type you have is important because the physiotherapy approach differs. Our Physiotherapist will clarify this during your assessment.

## Stress, urge or mixed: how are they different, and what comes first?

NICE asks clinicians to [sort leakage into stress, mixed or urgency incontinence at the first assessment and start treatment on that basis](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng123/chapter/Recommendations) (recommendation 1.3.1). In mixed incontinence, treatment is directed at whichever symptom is more troublesome.

| Type | Typical trigger | First-line approach | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Stress | Coughing, sneezing, laughing, running, jumping or lifting | Supervised pelvic floor muscle training for at least 3 months | [NICE NG123, 1.4.4](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng123/chapter/Recommendations) |
| Urge (urgency) | A sudden, strong need to pass urine, leaking on the way to the toilet | Bladder training for at least 6 weeks; a trial of cutting down caffeine | [NICE NG123, 1.4.1 and 1.4.11](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng123/chapter/Recommendations) |
| Mixed | Both of the above | Treat the predominant symptom first; pelvic floor training is also first-line | [NICE NG123, 1.3.1 and 1.4.4](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng123/chapter/Recommendations) |
| Overflow | Dribbling or constant leakage with a bladder that does not empty fully | Medical assessment by a doctor | [Singapore General Hospital](https://www.singhealth.com.sg/symptoms-treatments/urinary-incontinence) |

## What urinary incontinence treatment is available in Singapore, and in what order?

Singapore hospitals and international guidelines describe the same broad order: conservative care first, then medicines or procedures if that has not helped enough. [Singapore General Hospital](https://www.singhealth.com.sg/symptoms-treatments/urinary-incontinence) lists bladder training, pelvic floor exercises, medication and surgery, with surgery commonly considered for stress incontinence.

1. Assessment and simple changes. NICE recommends a [trial of caffeine reduction for an overactive bladder, adjusting fluid intake if it is very high or very low, and weight loss if your BMI is over 30](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng123/chapter/Recommendations) (1.4.1 to 1.4.3).

2. Pelvic floor muscle training for stress or mixed leakage. NICE recommends a supervised programme of at least 3 months, with at least 8 contractions 3 times a day, and continuing it if it helps (1.4.4, 1.4.5 and 1.4.7). A [Cochrane review of 31 trials (Dumoulin, Cacciari and Hay-Smith, 2018)](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD005654.pub4) concluded that pelvic floor muscle training can improve symptoms of stress and other types of incontinence, with rare and minor side effects. KKH notes that [improvement is usually seen only after 2 to 3 months](https://www.kkh.com.sg/symptoms-treatments/stress-urinary-incontinence-female-urinary-disorders).

3. Bladder training for urge or mixed leakage. This means emptying the bladder at regular intervals and learning techniques, such as distraction and relaxation, to manage the sense of urgency. NICE recommends at least 6 weeks as first-line treatment (1.4.11). KKH's physiotherapy programme for overactive bladder uses a [bladder diary, bladder retraining and pelvic floor exercises](https://www.kkh.com.sg/symptoms-treatments/physiotherapy-for-overactive-bladder-female-urinary-disorders).

4. Medicines, for urge symptoms. If bladder training alone is not enough, a doctor may add a medicine for overactive bladder (NICE 1.4.12). For stress incontinence, KKH states that there are at present no effective drugs.

5. Further options, decided with a specialist doctor. For an overactive bladder that has not responded to non-surgical treatment and medicines, NICE lists options such as botulinum toxin injections into the bladder wall and nerve stimulation after specialist review (1.4.15 and 1.4.44). For stress incontinence, see the next section.

## What surgery is there for female stress incontinence?

Surgery is an option for stress incontinence when non-surgical treatment has not worked well enough and you want to consider it. NICE says that if non-surgical management has failed, women should be offered a choice of [colposuspension, an autologous rectus fascial sling (using your own tissue), or a retropubic mid-urethral mesh sling](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng123/chapter/Recommendations), with extra safeguards around mesh (1.5.2). Injected bulking agents may be considered if those procedures are not suitable, though NICE notes they are less effective and the effect wears off over time (1.5.3). In Singapore, KKH's patient page describes [tension-free vaginal tape (TVT and TVT-O) and Burch colposuspension](https://www.singhealth.com.sg/symptoms-treatments/stress-urinary-incontinence-female-urinary-disorders) among the surgical options.

Why training first? The NICE committee noted that there is good evidence surgery can be more effective than pelvic floor muscle training for stress incontinence, but recommended training first because of the risks of surgery and the absence of side effects from training. Whether surgery suits you is a decision to make with a gynaecologist or urologist. Physiotherapy does not rule it out, and pelvic floor training is often part of the plan either way.

## When should I see a doctor about bladder leakage?

See your GP or a specialist rather than starting with physiotherapy alone if you have any of the following. These are drawn from the [Singapore General Hospital](https://www.singhealth.com.sg/symptoms-treatments/urinary-incontinence) patient page and NICE's [indications for specialist referral](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng123/chapter/Recommendations) (1.3.20 and 1.3.21):

- Blood in your urine, or pain when passing urine
- Repeated urinary tract infections
- Symptoms that start suddenly or get worse, or unexplained weight loss
- Ongoing bladder or urethral pain
- Difficulty emptying your bladder, or constant dribbling
- Bowel leakage as well as bladder leakage
- Signs of a possible neurological condition
- Leakage after previous continence surgery, pelvic cancer surgery or pelvic radiotherapy

If any of these apply, our Physiotherapist will ask you to see a doctor first, and can work alongside your doctor's plan afterwards.

## Is leaking after birth normal?

Leaking in the immediate postnatal period (weeks 1-6) is common, and a degree of spontaneous improvement is expected as the pelvic floor recovers from birth. However, leaking that persists beyond 3 months postpartum, or that is affecting your ability to exercise, return to work, or live without anxiety about accidents, warrants physiotherapy assessment. Waiting and hoping it resolves is a reasonable approach in the first 6-8 weeks; it is not a strategy beyond that point.

## Is doing "Kegel" exercises enough?

Pelvic floor exercises are an essential component of physiotherapy for stress incontinence - but "Kegels" done without assessment are only part of the picture. Many women squeeze rather than contract the correct muscles, or hold their breath, or have pelvic floor overactivity rather than weakness as their primary problem (in which case strengthening exercises may worsen symptoms). An assessment establishes what the pelvic floor is actually doing before prescribing a programme that addresses your specific presentation. NICE also recommends [confirming a correct pelvic floor contraction by examination before supervised training starts](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng123/chapter/Recommendations) (1.3.4).

## What does a session at Goji involve?

Our Physiotherapist will take a full bladder history - what you drink, how often you void, when you leak, and what triggers it. This provides an accurate picture of your bladder behaviour and helps distinguish between stress and urgency incontinence. The physical assessment typically includes:

- Pelvic floor muscle assessment: evaluating resting tone, ability to contract, ability to relax, endurance, and coordination. An internal vaginal assessment provides the most accurate information about pelvic floor function and is offered (but not required).

- Load testing: assessing how the pelvic floor responds under the demands that are relevant to you - coughing, jumping, lifting

- Screen for contributing factors: fluid intake, caffeine, bladder habits, and any ongoing hormonal factors (particularly relevant peri- and post-menopause)

You will leave with a programme specifically designed for your pelvic floor - not a generic leaflet - and a realistic understanding of the progress timeline.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long does physiotherapy take to improve urinary incontinence?

International guidelines recommend a minimum of 3 months of supervised pelvic floor training before assessing the outcome. Many women see meaningful improvement before that. Factors influencing the timeline include the severity of leakage, pelvic floor starting function, consistency of the home programme, and whether urgency incontinence is also present.

### Are there medicines for stress incontinence?

KK Women's and Children's Hospital states that there are at present no effective drugs for stress urinary incontinence, which is why pelvic floor training comes first and surgery is the main option if it is not enough. Medicines are used for urge symptoms from an overactive bladder, prescribed by a doctor.

### Can urinary incontinence happen even if I never had a vaginal birth?

Yes. Pregnancy itself - regardless of delivery mode - places significant load on the pelvic floor. Urinary incontinence also occurs in women who have never been pregnant, associated with high-impact exercise, chronic cough, or connective tissue factors. Pelvic floor physiotherapy is appropriate across all these presentations.

### I leak when I run or jump. Does that mean I should stop exercising?

Not necessarily, and not indefinitely. The goal of physiotherapy for exercise-associated leakage is to build pelvic floor capacity to the point where you can load it without leakage - not to avoid load permanently. In the short term, it may be appropriate to modify high-impact activity while building pelvic floor function. Our Physiotherapist will guide this specifically for your activity level and goals.

### Could my incontinence be related to prolapse?

Urinary incontinence and pelvic organ prolapse frequently co-occur, as they share common risk factors and the pelvic floor structures involved overlap. A full pelvic floor assessment will screen for both.

### Is urinary incontinence more common during perimenopause?

Yes. The decline in oestrogen during perimenopause affects the urethral and bladder tissue, reducing their elasticity and closure function. Both stress and urgency incontinence often worsen or appear for the first time during this transition. Physiotherapy addresses the muscular component; topical oestrogen (prescribed by a GP or gynaecologist) often supports tissue health in parallel.

### I've been leaking for years. Is it too late for physiotherapy to help?

No. The evidence supports pelvic floor physiotherapy regardless of how long incontinence has been present. Improvement may take longer in longstanding cases, but it remains the recommended first-line treatment before any surgical intervention is considered.
