## What is dry needling?

Dry needling uses thin, solid needles of the kind used in acupuncture, inserted into muscle and other soft tissue without injecting any medicine ([Dunning et al., 2014, Physical Therapy Reviews](https://doi.org/10.1179/108331913X13844245102034)). It is usually aimed at myofascial trigger points: tight, tender spots in a muscle that can be painful to press and may refer pain elsewhere.

## How is dry needling different from acupuncture?

Although both use similar thin needles, dry needling is based on anatomy and musculoskeletal assessment - targeting specific muscles and trigger points identified during your examination. Traditional acupuncture is rooted in a different framework based on meridians and energy flow. The needles look alike, but the reasoning behind where and why they are placed is different. Researchers studying dry needling describe the two as distinctly different in how they are applied and in their proposed mechanisms ([Boyce et al., 2020, International Journal of Sports Physical Therapy](https://doi.org/10.26603/ijspt20200103)).

| | Dry needling | Traditional acupuncture |
|---|---|---|
| Needle | Thin, solid, single-use | Thin, solid, single-use |
| Framework | Anatomy and musculoskeletal assessment | Meridians and energy flow |
| Where needles go | Muscles and trigger points found on examination | Acupuncture points along meridians |

## What is dry needling used for?

Physiotherapists may use dry needling to help with:

- Muscle tightness, spasm, and trigger-point-related pain
- Neck, shoulder, and lower back tension
- Tension-type headaches associated with neck and shoulder muscles
- Muscle-related symptoms in the upper or lower body accompanying sports and overuse injuries

Dry needling is chosen when your assessment suggests muscle tension or trigger points are contributing to your symptoms. It works best as part of an active plan rather than on its own.

## Does dry needling work?

The research is mixed. The clearest benefit is short-term pain relief; evidence for longer-term benefit is limited, and many of the studies are small or differ in how they were done.

| What was studied | What the reviews found |
|---|---|
| Musculoskeletal pain across body regions (36 systematic reviews) | Dry needling reduced pain in the short term compared with sham or no treatment, and was about as effective as other treatments. Limited data on mid- and long-term effects; results for physical function were contradictory ([Chys et al., 2023, Journal of Clinical Medicine](https://doi.org/10.3390/jcm12031205)) |
| Neck pain with trigger points, dry needling added to other treatment | Low-to-moderate evidence of short-term improvement in pain, disability and neck movement. No mid- or long-term effects were found ([Fernández-de-las-Peñas et al., 2021, Pain Research and Management](https://doi.org/10.1155/2021/8836427)) |
| Tension-type and cervicogenic headache, dry needling added to physiotherapy | Only three studies. Not enough evidence to strongly recommend it, but it may be a useful addition to physiotherapy ([France et al., 2014, Cephalalgia](https://doi.org/10.1177/0333102414523847)) |

The same umbrella review found that several studies showed an extra benefit when dry needling was combined with physiotherapy, compared with physiotherapy alone ([Chys et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.3390/jcm12031205)). This is why we use it alongside exercise and movement retraining, not in place of them.

## Is dry needling safe? What are the side effects?

When performed by a trained physiotherapist using sterile, single-use needles, dry needling is generally considered safe. Mild, short-lived side effects are common; serious ones are rare.

- In a study of 39 physiotherapists in Ireland covering 7,629 dry needling treatments, mild side effects followed about 19% of treatments. The most common were bruising, minor bleeding, and pain during or after treatment. Less common were a short flare of symptoms, drowsiness, headache, or nausea. No significant adverse events were reported ([Brady et al., 2014, Journal of Manual and Manipulative Therapy](https://doi.org/10.1179/2042618613Y.0000000044)).
- A larger study of 420 physical therapists and over 20,000 treatment sessions found minor side effects such as bleeding, bruising and pain were common, and major adverse events (such as a pneumothorax, a punctured lung) were rare ([Boyce et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.26603/ijspt20200103)).

Your physiotherapist will screen for any reasons it may not be suitable for you and will discuss what to expect. If you experience severe pain, obvious deformity, numbness, or an inability to move a limb at all, seek urgent medical assessment first.

## Who can do dry needling in Singapore?

In Singapore, physiotherapists must be registered with the Allied Health Professions Council (AHPC) and hold a valid Practising Certificate before they practise ([AHPC, Registers of Allied Health Professionals](https://www.ahpc.gov.sg/for-professionals/registers-of-allied-health-professionals/)). You can check a physiotherapist's registration on the [AHPC public register](https://prs.moh.gov.sg/prs/internet/profSearch/main.action?hpe=ahpc). The AHPC's description of physiotherapy practice does not mention dry needling specifically, so it is reasonable to ask a physiotherapist what dry needling training they have.

## Frequently asked questions

### Who offers dry needling at Goji?

### Does dry needling hurt?

Most people feel only a small prick as the needle enters, sometimes followed by a brief muscle ache or twitch. Discomfort is usually short-lived, and your physiotherapist will check in with you throughout.

### How will I feel afterwards?

Some people feel immediate relief or notice improved muscle length or activation, while others notice mild soreness for a day or so, similar to after exercise. Gentle movement and staying hydrated can help.

### How many sessions will I need?

Dry needling is not a fixed course - it is used when clinically appropriate as part of your wider plan. Your physiotherapist will discuss whether and how often it fits into your rehabilitation.

### Is it suitable for everyone?

Not always. Certain conditions, medications, or circumstances mean dry needling may not be recommended. Your physiotherapist will screen for these and suggest alternatives if needed.

### Will dry needling fix my problem on its own?

Dry needling can help ease muscle tension and pain, but it works best alongside exercise and movement retraining that address why the tension developed. It is one part of the plan, not a substitute for the rest of it.
