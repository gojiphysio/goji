You can, and after a caesarean it may make the first few days a little easier. Trials of abdominal binders after a C-section have mostly found slightly less pain and distress in the first 48 hours. What a binder won't do is close a tummy gap or shrink your tummy. There's no good evidence for either, so wear one if it makes you more comfortable and don't expect more from it than that.

## What does the research on binders actually show?

Almost all of the trials are in women who had a caesarean, and most are small. Taken together, they point to a modest benefit while the wound is fresh.

- A 2025 meta-analysis of 13 randomised trials found lower pain scores from 6 to 48 hours after surgery, less distress, a little more walking in the first day, and no extra complications ([Lin et al., 2025, Midwifery](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.midw.2024.104281)).
- An earlier analysis of six trials found lower pain scores at 24 and 48 hours ([Abd-ElGawad et al., 2021, International Journal of Gynaecology and Obstetrics](https://doi.org/10.1002/ijgo.13607)).
- Not every study agrees. A pooled analysis of four trials (601 women) found no clear difference in pain, though the women with binders reported less distress ([Di Mascio et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ejogrb.2021.05.014)). A Thai trial of 180 women found no difference in pain or in how far they could walk ([Chankhunaphas and Charoenkwan, 2020](https://doi.org/10.1080/01443615.2019.1631768)).

Where pain did drop, it was by roughly one point on a ten-point scale. You'd notice that getting out of bed, but it doesn't replace the pain relief your doctor prescribes. After a vaginal birth there's hardly any research, so we can't say either way.

On diastasis recti the answer is clearer. A 2023 review of 16 trials in postnatal women looked at exercise, binding, taping and electrical stimulation. None of them made a clinically meaningful difference to the width of the gap. Exercise narrowed it by about 0.4 cm on average and may help in other ways ([Benjamin et al., 2023, Physiotherapy](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.physio.2023.02.002)).

One small pilot trial of 32 first-time mothers did find that those who wore a binder felt a bit better about their body at six months ([Keshwani, Mathur and McLean, 2021](https://doi.org/10.1080/09593985.2019.1675207)). That matters to a lot of women. It just isn't the same thing as the muscles coming back together.

## Which binder claims hold up?

| What you'll hear | What the evidence says |
|---|---|
| It eases pain after a caesarean | Most pooled trials show a small drop in pain over the first 48 hours. Some trials found no difference. |
| It helps you get up and walk sooner | Some trials found more walking in the first day. Others found no change. |
| It closes diastasis recti | No. Binding hasn't been shown to make a meaningful difference to the gap. |
| It shrinks your tummy or gets your figure back | No trial shows this. One small pilot found a modest lift in body image. |
| It supports a weak core long term | Not shown. The muscles get stronger through exercise, not through being held in. |
| A traditional wrap speeds up recovery | Not scientifically proven, according to KKH. After a caesarean, wait about a month. |

## What about bengkung and other confinement wraps?

Wrapping the tummy is part of confinement for plenty of Singapore families. It might be a long bengkung cloth tied by a confinement lady or a velcro binder picked up from the pharmacy.

KK Women's and Children's Hospital lists the tight waist wrap among traditional Malay postnatal practices that haven't been scientifically proven. It also advises waiting a month after a caesarean before starting them, so the wound isn't disturbed ([HealthXchange, SingHealth](https://www.healthxchange.sg/pregnancy/postnatal/confinement-after-pregnancy-myths-facts)).

If wrapping is part of how your family looks after you, and it feels good, there's no need to fight over it. Feeling held and cared for in those early weeks counts for something. Just keep it loose enough to breathe deeply, and don't let anyone pull it tight to get your waist back.

## How should you wear a binder if you decide to?

- If the ward fitted one after your caesarean, follow their instructions first.
- Aim for snug, not tight. You should be able to take a full breath into your belly and ribs, and slide a flat hand under the edge.
- Wear it when you're up and about, if that's when it helps. Most women don't need it lying down.
- Put a thin cotton layer underneath. Our heat makes skin under elastic sweaty and sore quickly.
- Take it off a few times a day to let your skin breathe and to look at your wound.

As for how long, most of the trials measured the first two days. Nobody has shown a benefit from wearing one for months. A sensible approach is to use it while it makes everyday things like feeding, lifting and walking more comfortable, then drop it as you feel steadier. Some women go to a few hours a day first, then stop.

## What should you watch out for?

Loosen it or take it off if you notice any of these:

- It's hard to take a deep breath, or you feel numb, tingly or more sore than before
- Red marks that don't fade, a rash, or chafing
- A heavy or dragging feeling in the vagina, a bulge, or new leaking while you wear it

There's little research on binders and the pelvic floor specifically. Still, heaviness and leaking are worth getting checked whatever the cause. Our pages on [pelvic organ prolapse](/conditions/pelvic-organ-prolapse/) and [leaking after birth](/blog/is-leaking-after-birth-normal/) explain what's going on.

See your doctor promptly if your wound gets redder, hotter or more painful, leaks fluid or starts to open, or if you have a fever. The [postpartum recovery checklist](/blog/postpartum-recovery-checklist/) lists the signs that need a doctor urgently.

## When is it time to see a physio instead?

A binder holds you in. It doesn't tell you how your tummy and pelvic floor are actually working. Book an assessment if:

- You're still reaching for the binder weeks later because your tummy feels weak or unsupported
- You see doming or a ridge down the middle of your tummy when you sit up or lift your baby
- You have back or pelvic pain, leaking, or heaviness
- You want to get back to exercise and aren't sure where to start

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I wear a binder after a vaginal birth?

You can if it feels comfortable. Most of the research is on caesarean births, so there isn't good evidence either way for vaginal births. The same rules apply: snug not tight, and take it off if you feel heaviness or start leaking.

### Is a binder the same as shapewear or a waist trainer?

No. A postnatal binder is meant to support you while you heal. Shapewear and waist trainers are made to change how you look, and they're usually tighter. Nothing shows they speed up recovery, so if you wear one, keep it loose enough to breathe fully.

### Should I sleep in my binder?

Most women don't need to, since lying down takes the load off your tummy. If your surgical team gave you different advice, go with theirs.

### Can I do tummy and pelvic floor exercises while I'm wearing a binder?

Gentle breathing and pelvic floor exercises can usually start in the early days after birth, and a binder doesn't change that. Our guide to [pelvic floor exercises after birth](/blog/pelvic-floor-exercises-after-birth/) explains how to start.
