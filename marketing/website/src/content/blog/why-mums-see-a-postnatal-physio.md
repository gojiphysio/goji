Leaking, back pain and a gap in the tummy muscles. Of the 70 postnatal clients whose notes we counted, between our opening in June and the end of October 2026, 57% had leaking or bladder urgency written in their notes, 44% had back pain at their first visit and 40% had a tummy gap (diastasis recti) measured. The most common goal was getting back to exercise or sport.

These are counts from one clinic, of people who chose to see a physiotherapist, so they are not the rate for all mothers. They do show what tends to bring women through the door, and that none of these problems is unusual.

## What did we count, and how?

We counted from our own clinical notes. A script read each client's notes in memory, flagged a fixed list of keywords (for example "leaking", "SUI" or a diastasis measurement in finger-widths or centimetres), and printed totals only. No note text, names or dates left the script, and any count under five is shown as "<5" so no one can be picked out.

| What we looked at | Group | Count | Share |
|---|---|---|---|
| Leaking or bladder urgency in the notes | 70 postnatal clients | 40 | 57% |
| Back pain at the first visit | 70 postnatal clients | 31 | 44% |
| Tummy gap (diastasis recti) measured | 70 postnatal clients | 28 | 40% |
| Pelvic girdle pain at the first visit | 70 postnatal clients | 5 | 7% |
| Shoulder pain at the first visit | 70 postnatal clients | 5 | 7% |
| Neck or wrist pain at the first visit | 70 postnatal clients | <5 each | |

"Postnatal" here means the notes say the client had given birth. Keyword counting misses things written in unusual ways and can catch the odd false match, so read the shares as approximate.

## Is leaking really that common?

In our numbers, more than half of postnatal clients had leaking or urgency recorded. That is higher than in the general population of new mothers, which you would expect: people with a bladder problem are the ones who book a pelvic floor assessment. Across 24 studies of more than 35,000 women, about 31% had some urinary leaking between six weeks and one year after birth ([Moossdorff-Steinhauser et al., 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8295150/)).

Both numbers say the same thing: leaking after birth is common, and it is treatable. Supervised pelvic floor muscle training is the recommended first treatment for stress and mixed urinary incontinence ([NICE](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/qs77/chapter/Quality-statement-4-Supervised-pelvic-floor-muscle-training)). Our post on [whether leaking after birth is normal](/blog/is-leaking-after-birth-normal/) covers what tends to settle and what is worth getting assessed.

## Why does back pain come up so often after birth?

Back pain at the first visit was in 44% of postnatal notes, well ahead of neck, shoulder, wrist or pelvic girdle pain. The early months bring a lot of lifting and carrying, long stretches of feeding in one position, and abdominal muscles that are still recovering from pregnancy.

A physiotherapy assessment looks at how you are moving through those daily tasks as well as at the painful area, so the plan fits what your day actually involves. Pain in the pelvis at the front or back, around the pubic bone or sacroiliac joints, is a different problem with its own approach; our [pelvic girdle pain page](/conditions/pelvic-girdle-pain/) explains it.

## How many mums have a gap in their tummy muscles?

A tummy gap was measured in 40% of postnatal clients' notes. A gap between the two sides of the six-pack muscle is a normal adaptation in pregnancy, and it often narrows over the first year. A Norwegian study of 300 first-time mothers found a gap of two finger-widths or more in 60.0% of women at 6 weeks after birth and 32.6% at 12 months ([Sperstad et al., 2016, British Journal of Sports Medicine](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2016-096065)).

A measurement in the notes does not mean the gap was large or a problem, only that it was checked and recorded. Our [diastasis recti page](/conditions/diastasis-recti/) covers what is normal and what physiotherapy involves, and [diastasis recti: physio or surgery](/blog/diastasis-recti-physio-or-surgery/) covers when surgery is considered.

## What do mums want to get back to?

Across all 256 clients with notes, not only postnatal ones, 109 had a goal written down. Of those:

| Goal | Count | Share of clients with a goal |
|---|---|---|
| Exercise or sport (gym, Pilates, swimming and so on) | 40 | 37% |
| Work | 16 | 15% |
| Running | 12 | 11% |
| Lifting or carrying their baby or child without pain | 5 | 5% |
| Sleep | <5 | |

A client can have more than one goal, so the shares do not add up to 100%. Most goals were about getting back to something specific rather than only being pain-free, which is why an assessment usually ends with a plan built around that activity. ## Frequently asked questions

### Do I need a referral to see a postnatal physio?

No. You can book directly, and our [postpartum physiotherapy](/services/postpartum-physiotherapy/) page explains what the first session covers.

### Does having a caesarean mean I don't need to think about my pelvic floor?

No. Pregnancy itself loads the pelvic floor, so pelvic floor problems can follow a caesarean birth as well as a vaginal one. Our [caesarean recovery page](/conditions/caesarean-recovery/) covers recovery after a C-section.

### Why do your numbers look higher than research figures?

Because the people we see have chosen to see a physiotherapist, often for one of these problems. Research figures come from all women after birth, whether or not they had symptoms or sought help.

### Were my notes read to write this post?

No person read notes for this post. A script counted keywords in memory and printed totals only, with counts under five hidden, so no one can be identified from these numbers.
